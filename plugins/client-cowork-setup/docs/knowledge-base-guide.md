# Your knowledge base, {{CLIENT_NAME}}

For the owner of the knowledge base, and anyone who works in it. Read once; after that,
`/guide` answers the rest.

## What it is

Your working folder, the one the build day set up, with what the business knows written
down once. Every fact sits on one page, with where it came from, who owns it and the
date it is next checked. Nothing moves and nothing new sits beside your folders: each
folder's README grows into a card that says what is in it, what is not, what changed
last, and what it links to.

Open the folder in Obsidian, a free app, and you see it as a map. Each kind of folder has
its own colour: clients, offers, decisions, suppliers, policies. The lines show how they
connect: which offers each client bought, which decisions changed which prices, which
suppliers an operation depends on.

## The sections, and how they link

Every business keeps much the same kinds of thing under its own folder names. At setup
you agree which of your folders is which kind, drop any you do not have, and add any of
your own.

| Kind | What it holds | It must link to |
|---|---|---|
| Company | Who you are, what this year is for | |
| Offers | What you sell, how it is priced and delivered | Its price, and how it is delivered |
| Clients | One folder per client | What they bought, who you deal with, decisions about them |
| Pipeline | Prospects not yet clients | The client folder, the day they sign |
| People | Your team, and the people you deal with | |
| Operations | How the work gets done | The suppliers it depends on |
| Suppliers | Who you buy from, and on what terms | |
| Finance | Pointers to your accounts system and the rules for money; never balances | The system that holds the figures |
| Marketing | Content, the website, case studies; the public part | Only the offer it shows |
| Decisions | One page per decision: what, when, who, why | Everything it changed |
| Policies | Your standing rules, including what never goes in | The folders each rule governs |
| Library | Reusable material that names no client | |

Some links are never allowed, and the weekly check reports them first: anything public
pointing at anything private, one client's folder pointing at another's, and any figure
from your accounts copied into a page.

## How things get in

- **`/capture`** files a document, email, transcript or note exactly as it came, in the
  folder it belongs to. It is marked unreviewed.
- **`/compile`** turns what has been captured into pages. A changed fact keeps its old
  version underneath, dated. Two sources that disagree are both shown, for you to settle.
- **The weekly run** does both for you, for what changed in your folders and connected
  tools that week.

A new page needs two independent sources, or a decision behind it. One remark in one
email waits for its second source.

## The ten minutes that keep it true

Once a week, an hour before the review you already do, a list waits for the owner:
`/ember-review`. New pages, pages due a check, disagreements, questions it could not
answer, and anything the monthly check proposes to tidy or retire. You answer each line
yes, no, or replace with the right fact. Fifteen lines at most.

Nothing becomes company knowledge until the owner says yes. No scheduled run can mark a
page checked.

Once a month it checks pages about money and customers against the systems that own
them, publishes four numbers on its health, and proposes what to cut so its own
instructions stay lean: an old correction folded into a rule, turned into a skill, or
retired.

## Asking it

Type `/ask`, or just ask. Every claim comes with the page behind it, and one line on how
sure it is and how old the page is. When the answer is not there it says "I don't know"
and tells you where to find it. Give it the answer and say "put that in": it is
captured and comes to the next review.

## Client material, case studies and content

Client material is the most valuable thing in the knowledge base and the easiest to
leak. It reaches a case study, a post, the website, a talk, or work for another client
only through **`/release`**:

1. It names every page the draft drew on.
2. It lists everything that could identify the client: names, people, places, products,
   exact figures, quotes, and details that together point to one business.
3. It reads the client's card for recorded consent. Consent counts only when the email
   or signed form that gave it is filed in their folder, and only for the use it covers.
4. It removes or blurs everything consent does not cover. Exact figures become ranges.
5. It shows the owner the text beside what it removed. Only the owner's yes files it in
   the public folder, with a record of what was released, on what basis, and who agreed.

It never publishes, posts or sends; a person does that. A change to released wording
is a new release. Where your rules on AI or personal data are stricter, they win.

## Governance first

Before a single page is written, setup looks for any rules you already have: an AI
policy, a data protection impact assessment, a manifesto, a rule pack. Your stricter
rules win, line by line. With none, it asks three questions (what personal data goes in,
who can see it, what never goes in) and writes a basic rules page and a never list.

The never list cannot be shortened: passwords and logins, bank and card details,
identity numbers, health, pay, and anything given in confidence. Each connected tool is
set to read only where the tool allows, so the limits are in the tool, not only in the
instructions. Setup drafts a note for whoever looks after data protection, saying the
knowledge base exists and what it holds. It does not send it.
