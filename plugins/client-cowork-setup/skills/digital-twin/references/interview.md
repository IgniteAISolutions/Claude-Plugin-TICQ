# The extraction interview

Run against a live instance of the task. Never in the abstract. The person only hears
questions about their job. Assemble the five parts in `skill-spec.md` as you go, and
never show them.

One question at a time. Follow the thread when something interesting appears, then
come back. Push on vagueness: "I tidy it up" is not an answer, and "I move the numbers
into a table and cut anything that is not a decision" is.

## Opening: the walk

"Walk me through what happens right now, from the very beginning. What makes you think
'right, I need to do this'?"

Let them talk. They will jump around. You are listening for the structure underneath.

"What do you open first?"
"Is there anything you look up during it? Prices, previous examples, a template, a
system?"
"Are there points where you make a judgement call? Like 'if the value is over ten
thousand I handle it differently'?"
"What do you do when you are done? Where does it go, and who picks it up?"

## The reasoning, not the preferences

This is the block that makes the skill intelligent rather than obedient. For every
preference they state, ask why, and for every rule they state, ask what happened.

"You said you always put the summary first. What happened the time it was not first?"
"You said you never include that. What went wrong when it was included?"
"Who taught you to do it that way, and what were they reacting to?"

The reasoning lets the skill make good calls in cases the instructions do not cover.
Preferences without reasoning produce a skill that follows the letter and misses the
point.

## The example

"Can you show me the best one of these you have ever produced? Not a perfect one. One
you were happy with."

Then open it. Do not accept a description. If they cannot find one, go to the folder
where these live, read three, and watch which one they react to. This is the
highest-value part of the skill and the first thing cut when the room runs late. Do
not let it be cut.

"Now show me one that was not great, or tell me about a time the output missed. What
was wrong with it?"

Bad examples reveal the non-obvious standards, the things the person takes for granted
until they see them violated.

## The never list

"Is there anything that should absolutely never be in it? Phrases, assumptions,
formatting, a kind of content. Things that would make you look at it and immediately
think 'no, that's wrong'?"

Taste is what somebody rejects. Most of a useful skill is what it must never do.

## Rules of thumb

"Are there rules of thumb you follow, things you just know from experience? 'If the
client asked for X they usually also need Y.' 'Always round up on lead times.'"

"Is there anyone, a colleague or a competitor, who does this really well? What
specifically do you like about how they do it?"

## Done

"When one of these is finished properly, what does that look like? How do you know it
is done?"

This is the question that makes a skill good rather than plausible. Without it the
skill polishes forever, or stops at the first draft.

## Authority

Read the tier from the frame. Then:

"Is there any point in this where you would want to be asked before it goes ahead?
Anything that touches money, a client, or a record in a system?"

Write their answer into the skill in their words.

## Closing

Say back the task in one paragraph, as prose, in their terms. Ask what is wrong. Then
build version one and run it on the second live case, in front of them.
